Obligations, before anything about maturity

These are not scores and they are not our opinion. Each is something in force today that we found is not in place. They sit above the rest of this document because they are not aspirations.

UK GDPR Art 22C — in force 5 Feb 2026 (Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 s.80)

For any significant decision made without meaningful human involvement: are the four safeguards documented — inform, representations, human intervention, contest — no.

Article 22 was replaced in February. The regime moved from prohibition-with-exceptions to permitted-with-documented-safeguards, so the burden is now evidencing the four. Test whether the human reviewer can actually overturn — the ICO's March 2026 recruitment report found rubber-stamping presented as oversight.

UK GDPR — in force

Is there a current DPIA for each AI use touching personal data — no.

Unchanged by the reforms, and the ICO can compel production of them.

SM&CR — in force

Is a named Senior Manager accountable for AI outcomes in their business area — no.

Accountability sits with the business SMF, not with technology and not with the vendor.

Consumer Duty — in force

Have AI-influenced customer decisions been tested against the four Consumer Duty outcomes — no.

Not a new rule; an existing rule applied to a new object. The Mills Review (Jul 2026) declined to recommend AI-specific regulation precisely because this already bites.

We are not lawyers and this is not legal advice. It is a screen against what is currently in force, run so that these are on the table rather than discovered later. Anything deferred by the Digital Omnibus to December 2027 or August 2028 is deliberately excluded — a readiness report that scores you against obligations that do not yet apply is one your own risk function will pull apart.

04

How to read the scores

Every dimension is scored from 1 to 5 on the same scale. The stages describe how deliberate and repeatable a practice is, not how much technology is in use.

1 · Exploringcurious, nothing running
2 · Experimentingpilots, no repeatable path
3 · Operationalisingfirst things running for real
4 · Scalingrepeatable, spreading, measured
5 · AI-nativehow the business works

Four dimensions carry half as much weight again as the others — data, the reach of AI into your own knowledge, the quality of your internal platforms, and how strongly your work is driven by real users. These are the ones the research finds everything else depends on. The weights are shown against each dimension.

How we arrived at each number. Every dimension was scored twice, by different means: our assessment against a written description of each stage, and a checklist of things that either exist in your organisation or do not — a published policy, named data owners, prompts under version control, a log of what your automations have done. Where the two disagreed we have taken the midpoint and said so in the text, rather than reporting the more comfortable of the two.

Why those two numbers differ, and why we show both. The first is what the time actually costs you today, if AI removed all of it and everybody used it. The second applies three discounts: how many people will really adopt it, how much of the task it will really do, and how cleanly it will really connect. The evidence is that the first number overstates by somewhere between two and ten times. Most business cases quote it anyway.

And neither of them is your first year. Value arrives on a curve, not a step. There is a period where people are learning to work differently, where output has to be checked more carefully than it eventually will be, and where testing and approval are absorbing more change than they were built for. Published research finds this dip consistent enough to plan around. Our working assumption — and we want to be clear it is an assumption rather than a measurement — is that the dip runs deeper and longer where the foundations are weaker, which is what the first half of this document assesses. On that basis we have modelled roughly 35% of steady-state value in year one, or £36,930 against a year-one cost of £297,500. Change that percentage and the case changes with it; it is the single assumption most worth arguing about.

Two things follow. The return is a second-year story and should be funded as one. And the common failure is not that the value never arrives — it is that the dip is read as failure and the funding is pulled while the return is still coming. One further cost is not in these numbers at all: the same research finds change failure rate tends to rise as more change moves faster through pipelines and review gates built for less of it. We have not modelled it because we have not measured your current failure rate, but it should be budgeted rather than discovered.

How we assessed this

So that you can weigh the findings, and challenge them.

  • 10 interviews across leaders & sponsors, technical & engineering, practitioners, alongside direct observation of how work is done.
  • 30 of 30 questions answered across 15 dimensions, of which 48% were confirmed against something we could see rather than taken on description alone.
  • Nothing here is self-reported. No survey was circulated for your teams to score themselves.
  • One workflow examined in detail, and 4 candidate use cases scored, costed and ranked against your own numbers.

What this assessment does not cover. It examines your organisation, one workflow and 4 candidates inside it. It does not assess your other workflows, and the figures are a business case rather than an audited saving — they rest on the baseline your own people gave us, and no code was audited. We would rather say so than imply otherwise.

Where the framework comes from. Seven of the 15 dimensions and their relative weights are adapted from published 2025 research into the capabilities that predict whether AI investment produces results; that research measures amplification rather than maturity, so the stages, the ceiling rule and the sequencing are ours. The governance dimension is additionally anchored to the ISO/IEC 42001:2023 Annex A control objectives and the NIST AI RMF core functions, so that what we found there can be carried into a certification or assurance programme rather than sitting beside one. That anchoring is at objective and function level; it is not a control-level Statement of Applicability and does not constitute an audit.
