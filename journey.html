Journey map — how we run an assessment

What you are actually doing

Finding out whether AI would work here, where it would be worth pointing, and what has to be true first. Not building anything. The output is a decision, and sometimes the decision is "not yet" — that is a successful engagement, not a failed one.

Three things make it go wrong, and they are all avoidable. Asking leadership what the team can do instead of asking the team. Believing a written policy over what people actually do. And quoting a value number without the discount that makes it realistic.

Which of the three you are running

Ready? — someone wants to know whether their organisation can take AI at all. No specific idea yet. Two weeks, and the output is a readiness picture plus a plan.
Validate — someone has one idea and wants to know if it is any good and what it is worth. About a week, and the output is a prototype and a costed case.
Prioritise — a department has lots of ideas and needs to know which ones and in what order. Two to four weeks, and the output is a ranked backlog.

If you are unsure, run Ready?. Sizing a use case in an organisation that cannot approve, host or run it wastes everybody's time.

The words this uses that you probably do not know yet

Target stage — where the client wants to be in twelve months, on a 1–5 scale. Agree it before scoring anything; it sets the bar everything else is measured against.
The ceiling — the lowest-scoring dimension. It caps the whole assessment, because an organisation cannot run AI more maturely than its weakest foundation allows.
The capping dimension — whichever one is setting that ceiling. Usually where the recommendation starts.
Cohorts — leaders, practitioners, technical. Skills are scored separately for each, because the average hides the gap that matters.
Confirmed / told / guessed — how you know each answer. Confirmed means you saw it. Told means someone said it. Guessed means you inferred it. The report prints the mix, so be honest.
Spine and checklist — every dimension is scored twice: your judgement, and a list of things that either exist or do not. When they disagree, that gap is usually the finding.
Potential vs realistic value — potential is what the time costs today. Realistic applies three discounts: adoption, effectiveness, integration. The gap is normally two to ten times.
The probe — running ten real examples through the idea to see how many produce something credible. Include the awkward ones; clean examples prove nothing.
Run capability — the ten things somebody has to be able to do the day after go-live. The question is not whether it can be built, but whether it survives you leaving.
The gates — approvals a thing must pass to reach production. Ask who decides, what evidence they need, and whether anyone has actually spoken to them.
Overwatch — the automated audit of a client's own code. Only relevant where they own code, and it needs read access granted in writing.
Exposure vs maturity gap — a maturity gap is something not yet good enough. An exposure is something already happening that carries risk today. Exposures do not wait for the roadmap.

If you work inside a bigger organisation

These journeys are written as though you have an executive sponsor who can unblock things and summon anyone. Most people running this do not. If you are assessing your own team or department, three things change.

  • Assess what you can see, and mark the rest N/A. A dimension marked not assessed is excluded from the score and named in the report. That is honest. Inventing a number is not, and the weights renormalise around whatever you leave out.
  • A question you could not answer is a finding. "Nobody could tell us who owns AI risk" is a real result, not a hole in your work — and it is often the most important sentence in the report.
  • The gaps become your ask. The list of what you could not reach is the case for the access you need. Take it to leadership as the output rather than apologising for it.

Every data row below has a fourth column — if you can't get it — with a proxy source or an honest way to record the gap. None of them require authority you do not have.

Before you start

  • Open the tool and load a worked example first. Twenty minutes reading a completed assessment teaches more than any briefing.
  • Agree the target stage with whoever is sponsoring this, in writing, before you score anything.
  • Book the conversations now. Every overrun traces back to interviews not booked early.
  • Decide what you will do if the answer is "not yet", before you find out.
